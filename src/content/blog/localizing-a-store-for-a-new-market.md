A brand does well in one country and decides to try another. The product page gets translated, the ads get translated, and the campaign goes live.

Then the numbers come in lower than at home, and nobody's quite sure why. The words are correct. The page looks the same. It just doesn't sell the same.

Usually it's because the page was translated, not localized. Every word is right, but it still feels like it was made for someone else.

## What shoppers notice

People don't read a product page the way a translator does. They scan it for signals that this shop is for them. A few small things give it away fast:

- **Prices in the wrong currency,** or in the right currency but written the wrong way. "€49.00" and "49,00 €" are the same price, but only one looks local in most of Europe.
- **Sizes and units from somewhere else.** US shoe sizes, inches and pounds on a page aimed at Europe.
- **Payment options they don't use.** If the only choices are card and PayPal, you're missing what a lot of people actually pay with. In Sweden that's often Klarna or Swish. In the Netherlands it's iDEAL.
- **Delivery that doesn't say where from.** "Ships in 3–5 business days" doesn't tell a Swedish shopper whether that's from Stockholm or from another continent.
- **Reviews from another country.** Reviews written in English, in dollars, from people in Texas, don't build much trust with someone in Gothenburg.

Any one of these is small. Together, they make the page feel like it wasn't built for the person reading it, even if they can't say exactly what feels off.

## Tone matters as much as the words

Direct translation also carries over the tone, and tone doesn't always travel.

Copy that works in the US can feel loud elsewhere. Lots of exclamation marks, "INCREDIBLE", "you won't believe". In some markets that reads as enthusiasm. In others it reads as a sales pitch, and people put their guard up.

A good translator adjusts for this. A machine translation usually doesn't. It keeps the same energy, in a new language, and the result can sound slightly off to native speakers.

## What to change, besides the words

Here's what I'd check before putting ad money behind a page in a new market.

**1. Currency and number format.** Show the local currency, and use the local format for prices, decimals and thousands. Check the cart and checkout too, not just the product page.

**2. Sizes and units.** Use the sizing system people know. If the product has a size guide, localize that as well.

**3. Payment methods.** Offer the ones people in that country expect. Most store platforms and payment providers support the common local methods, so this is often a settings change, not a rebuild.

**4. Delivery and returns.** Be specific: where it ships from, how long it takes to that country, what it costs, and how returns work. Vague delivery info is one of the most common reasons people hesitate.

**5. Reviews and proof.** Even a handful of reviews from local customers helps more than hundreds from somewhere else. If you don't have any yet, it's worth getting some early.

**6. Trust signals.** Local contact details, local customer service hours, a returns address in the region if you have one. Generic trust badges don't do much on their own.

**7. The checkout.** People often check the product page and forget the checkout is a separate set of text, labels and emails. Shipping notices, order confirmation emails and error messages all need translating too.

## Get a native speaker to read it

Machine translation is fine for a first draft. It's not fine for a page you're paying to send people to.

Before launch, have a native speaker read the whole page, the checkout and the emails. Ask them two things: does anything read strangely, and would they buy from this page? The second question is the important one.

If you can, have them click through the full purchase on their phone. They'll catch things a translator never sees, like a button label that's cut off because the word is longer in their language.

## Ads and the page should match

One more thing. If the ad is localized but the landing page isn't, or the other way round, people notice the switch. Someone who clicks a Swedish ad and lands on a page with dollar prices will often leave in the first few seconds.

Treat the ad, the page and the checkout as one thing that all needs to feel local.

## A quick checklist

Before launching in a new country:

- local currency and number format, everywhere
- local sizes and units
- local payment methods at checkout
- clear delivery times and costs to that country
- returns explained simply
- some reviews from local customers
- a native speaker has read the page, checkout and emails
- the ad and the page match

None of this is complicated. It's just easy to skip when the translation already looks finished.
