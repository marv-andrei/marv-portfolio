The most expensive landing page mistakes are almost never about design. They're small, boring things: a button that goes nowhere, a discount code that says "invalid", the cart adding the wrong size.

They're expensive because they usually get found after the ads are already running. By the time someone notices, you've paid for a few days of clicks that had no chance of turning into sales.

You only catch these by clicking through the page like a customer would. Here's the list I go through before a page goes live.

## 1. Every button goes somewhere

Click every button and every link. All of them, including the ones in the footer and the ones that repeat further down the page.

On long pages it's normal to have the same button five or six times. When a section gets duplicated during the build, one of those buttons often keeps a placeholder link, or points to a test version of the checkout.

## 2. The price and discount match the ad

Put the ad and the page side by side. If the ad says 50% off, the page should say 50% off, near the top, in the same words.

People click an ad because of one specific promise. If they can't find it within a few seconds, most of them won't go looking for it. They'll go back to scrolling.

## 3. It works on a phone first

Most paid traffic lands on a phone. Check the page on an actual phone, not just a narrow browser window on your laptop.

Look for text that's too small, buttons that are hard to tap, images that crop awkwardly, and pop-ups that cover the whole screen with a close button you can't reach.

## 4. Every variant adds the right item

If the product comes in sizes, colours or bundles, add each one to the cart and check what actually lands there.

This one catches a lot of people. The page shows "Black, Size M" but the cart gets "White, Size S", because the variant IDs were copied from another product.

## 5. Discount codes apply at checkout

If the page mentions a code, test that code at checkout. Check that it applies to the right products, that it hasn't expired, and that it works together with any automatic discounts.

A shopper who types in a code and sees "invalid" is gone, and they don't usually come back.

## 6. Images are compressed

Large images are the most common reason a landing page loads slowly. A single uncompressed photo can be several megabytes.

Run the page through a speed test, or just open it on mobile data instead of wifi. If you're watching blank space while it loads, so is everyone you're paying to send there.

## 7. Forms send a real test lead

Fill in every form with real details and follow the lead through. Does it arrive in the CRM? Does the confirmation email send? Does the right person get notified?

A form can look perfect and still send its submissions nowhere. I've written more about this in [what happens after someone clicks "Book a call"](/blog/after-book-a-call/).

## 8. Translations read naturally

If the page is in another language, have a native speaker read it before launch. Machine translation gets the words right but often gets the tone wrong, and shoppers notice even when they can't say exactly why.

Check the small stuff too: currency format, sizes, delivery times, and payment options people in that market actually use.

## 9. The headlines alone tell the story

Scroll through the page and read only the headlines. Do they make sense on their own? Do they tell you what the product is, why it's different, and what to do next?

Plenty of visitors only skim. If the headlines don't carry the message, a large part of your audience never gets it.

## How to actually do this

The trick is to do it as a customer, not as the person who built the page. You know where everything is, so it's easy to skip past problems without noticing.

A few things that help:

- **Do it on your phone,** on mobile data, with a fresh browser (or a private window).
- **Ask someone who hasn't seen the page** to buy something from it while you watch. Don't help them.
- **Keep the list somewhere you'll reuse it.** A shared doc or a checklist in your project tool. Add to it every time something slips through.

## Why it's worth the time

It takes half an hour at most. Compare that to a day of ad spend sent to a page with a broken checkout button.

None of these checks need a developer. They just need someone to sit down, slow down, and click through the page one more time before anyone pays to send traffic to it.
