An advertorial is a sales page that reads like an article. Someone clicks an ad, lands on something that looks like a story or a piece of news, and by the end they've been introduced to a product and given a reason to buy it.

When they work, they work well, especially for products that need a bit of explaining. When they don't, it's usually because someone treated it as a longer product page and skipped the parts that make people trust it.

Here's the structure I follow, using a made-up product as the example: a seat cushion for people who sit at a desk all day.

## 1. The hook

The headline and first few lines need to sound like news or a story, not an ad.

Compare:

- "The Best Ergonomic Seat Cushion, 50% Off Today"
- "Why physiotherapists keep telling office workers to change one thing about their chair"

The first one tells the reader they're being sold to. The second makes them curious. That's the job of the hook: get them to read the next line.

Keep it believable. A hook that overpromises might get the click, but the reader feels let down by the second paragraph.

## 2. The problem

Describe the problem the way the reader experiences it, in their words.

Not "lumbar spine compression causes discomfort". Something closer to: "By 3pm your lower back aches, you're shifting in your chair every few minutes, and standing up after a long meeting feels like it takes a second longer than it should."

If they recognise themselves here, they keep reading. This section is about them, not the product. The product hasn't been mentioned yet.

## 3. What they've already tried

This is the part most advertorials skip, and it's often the reason they don't convert.

Your reader has almost certainly tried something already. A new chair. Stretching. A rolled-up towel behind their back. Standing desks they stopped using after a week.

Name those things and explain, fairly, why they didn't fully work. This does two jobs. It shows you understand their situation. And it stops your product from feeling like "one more thing" they'll try and give up on.

Skip this section and the product arrives as just another gimmick.

## 4. The discovery

Now the product comes in, as the answer to everything above.

Explain what it is and, more importantly, why it works where other things didn't. Tie it back to the problem and the failed fixes. "Unlike a cushion that just adds padding, this one…"

Keep it simple. One or two clear reasons it's different are better than a list of twelve features.

## 5. Proof

By now the reader is interested but probably sceptical. This section is where they decide whether to believe you.

Good proof includes:

- **Reviews** from real customers, ideally with specifics ("I noticed it by the end of the first week") rather than "Great product!"
- **How it works,** in plain language, maybe with a simple diagram
- **Comparisons** with the alternatives they mentioned in section 3
- **Numbers** you can actually back up: how many customers, ratings, how long the company's been around

Only use claims you can support. One honest review does more than a made-up statistic.

## 6. The offer

Now be clear and direct. What does it cost, what's the deal, and what happens if it doesn't work for them?

A good offer section answers:

- the price, and any discount
- what they get (sizes, colours, bundles)
- the guarantee or returns policy
- delivery time and cost
- why now (a real reason, like a limited stock run or an ending promotion, not a fake countdown that resets on refresh)

The guarantee matters more than people think. It removes the last reason to hesitate.

## 7. The call to action

The button should be easy to find, and it should say what happens next. "Get my cushion" is clearer than "Submit" or "Learn more".

Repeat it. On a long page, people decide to buy at different points. Put a button after the discovery section, after the proof, and at the end, so nobody has to scroll back up to find it.

## When it doesn't convert

If an advertorial isn't working, go through it section by section and ask:

- **Hook:** does it sound like an ad? Is it believable?
- **Problem:** would your customer describe it this way?
- **Failed fixes:** is this section there at all?
- **Discovery:** is it clear why this is different?
- **Proof:** would a sceptic believe it?
- **Offer:** is the price, guarantee and delivery clear?
- **Call to action:** can they find the button without scrolling back?

Most of the time, one step is missing or rushed. In my experience it's usually the "what they've already tried" part.

## The order matters

It's tempting to move the offer up the page so people see it sooner. But an advertorial works because it earns the right to sell. Put the price before the reader cares about the problem, and you've just built a product page with extra steps.
